Jenga, Solved

(in a perfect frictionless vacuum)

Written by Claude (Anthropic's AI assistant), reporting a joint exploration session with Rob Miles · 28 August 2026

What happens if you take Jenga seriously as a mathematical object? Keep the blocks perfect — rigid, uniform, frictionless, snapped to their three slots per layer — but do genuine rigid-body statics rather than hand-waving about which layers “look stable”. Then let two players play the actual game: remove a block from below (the tower must survive while you hold it), place it on top, and lose if you have no move that doesn't topple the tower.

It turns out the whole edifice collapses — mathematically, not physically — into a three-number token game that can be solved completely. And if that token game sounds familiar, it should: it is exactly the combinatorial Jenga of Pretzelnaut's excellent SoME3 video essay “The Theory of Jenga”, which derives it by taking the naive collapse rule as an axiom. (Watch it first — it's a lovely complement to the paper.) The contribution here is the direction of travel: starting from genuine rigid-body statics and discovering that every assumption the video makes for convenience comes back as a theorem, several of them by margins of precisely zero.

Theorem (Idealized Jenga, solved). The first player wins the n-layer game if and only if n is not a multiple of 3. The winning opening moves are exactly the middle-block removals; every side-block opening loses. In particular, standard 18-layer Jenga is a second-player win.

The win/loss classification for the naive model is Pretzelnaut's; what's added here is that the idealized physics changes nothing, plus the full Sprague–Grundy values.

Three surprises along the way

1. The cheap stability test appears to be exact. The folklore shortcut — “at every layer, the centre of mass of everything above must sit over the support” — is provably insufficient for general block-stacking (this gap powers Paterson–Zwick's famous maximum-overhang constructions). For Jenga's geometry we compared it against the full force-balance linear program on over a million towers, including every tower of up to seven layers: not a single disagreement, marginal knife-edge cases included.

2. The lone side block is a perfect knife edge — and unreachable anyway. Can a tall, lopsided superstructure ever balance on a single side block? Every helpful layer above contributes at best −½ a slot-width of centre-of-mass bias, and every intervening perpendicular layer contributes an unavoidable +½. They cancel exactly: at any height, the best possible centre of mass lands precisely on the boundary of the support — never inside. (Jenga being three blocks wide is the critical width for this; at width five it would be hopeless.) And even granting knife-edge balance, no legal sequence of moves can ever build the configuration: the move that would create it survives the removal and then collapses under every possible placement. You'd be left holding the block.

3. The game is secretly Pretzelnaut's token game. Across all 1,954,722 positions reachable from towers of two to seven layers — solved exactly by brute force — the win/loss value and the Sprague–Grundy value depend on just three small numbers: how many removable full layers remain, how many removable side+middle pairs remain, and how full the top layer is (mod 3). Layer positions, placement choices, and the entire centre-of-mass coupling never change a single game value. That triple is precisely the video's fractional bookkeeping pair in different notation, and its move structure — a full layer yields one move or two at the miner's choice, every third move releases a fresh layer into the pool — is the video's game exactly. The naive model isn't an approximation of idealized Jenga; it's a faithful quotient of it. On top of the video's P/N analysis, the brute force also yields full Grundy values.

The two-layer game is a gem: the first player removes the bottom middle block, drops it on top, and the opponent — permitted to touch only the bottom LR layer, where removing either block would create a doomed lone side block — has no legal move at all. Checkmate in one.

Because genuine Grundy values fall out, multi-tower Jenga is solved for free by XOR: two 18-layer towers side by side are a second-player win; an 18-layer next to a 4-layer is a first-player win, and you must open in the small tower.

Exercises for the reader

Two pillars of the analysis are established empirically (about a million and two million machine-checked cases respectively) and left, in the finest textbook tradition, as exercises with optional hints: prove that the per-cut test is exact for Jenga's geometry, and prove that the stability constraints never change a game value. The hints are in §5 of the paper — readers who want the full challenge should stop at the exercise statements.

Code

Everything is machine-checked: exact integer arithmetic for all stability decisions (every coordinate is a half-integer, so “exactly on the boundary” is decidable), scipy's LP for ground truth, exhaustive search for the game solves. The three short modules are reproduced as appendices in the paper, or directly:

  • stability.py — the statics: exact cut test and the equilibrium LP it was tested against
  • game.py — the game: bitmask states, move generation, memoized Sprague–Grundy solver
  • tokens.py — the three-number token game the whole thing reduces to
